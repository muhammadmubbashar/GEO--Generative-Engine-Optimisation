Slide 5

Both work the same way: a question goes in, pages are found, an AI writes an answer and cites sources. ChatGPT uses the live web, which changes daily; we cannot see what it read or swap Flight Centre's page. Our sandbox uses a frozen copy of real Brave search results (50 questions, 953 pages), the Luna model (gpt-5.6-luna) through the OpenAI API, and swaps only Flight Centre's page between original and improved. So any change in citations comes from our page changes. 5

Slide 6

This is how the sandbox is built. Stage 1 freezes Brave Search results through its API. Stage 2 downloads each page without running JavaScript and turns it into plain text with Python and BeautifulSoup. Stage 3 is our own code that builds the improved Flight Centre pages, with automated tests for each change. Stage 4 builds the source pack: 5 to 10 numbered pages per question, in two shuffled orders. Stage 5 sends the question and pages to the OpenAI API, model gpt-5.6-luna, which must cite sources by number. Stage 6 maps each citation back to its page and records whether Flight Centre was cited. Everything is saved and fingerprinted. Performance: each page is capped at 24,000 characters; each comparison is 580 paid API answers; failed API calls resume without losing results; and every result can be recounted offline. 6

Slide 8

An AI can only cite what it can read. Our methods do not add new claims; they make Flight Centre's existing information easier for the AI to find, read and understand. They work in three ways. Expose: some deal details only load with JavaScript, and FAQ questions sit inside clickable buttons, so a tool reading the page text misses them; we make them visible. Organise: key facts such as route, starting fare and fees are put in clear labelled fields. Connect: each FAQ question is kept beside its answer, so the answer keeps its meaning. Each change was tested on its own against the original page, and the most promising ones were combined into one final page. 8

Slide 9

Both versions get the same question, the same competitor pages in the same order, and the same AI model. The only difference is whether Flight Centre's page is the original or the improved one. AI answers vary from run to run, so each question is answered 10 times: 2 source orders times 5 repeats. Across the 29 questions where Flight Centre had a page in the results, that gives 290 answers per version. We count how many answers cite Flight Centre in each version and compare them. The AI is never told to cite Flight Centre. 9

Slide 10

Problem: Flight Centre's deal cards only appear after JavaScript runs. Many AI tools read a page without running JavaScript, so in the text the AI received, the "Hot deals" heading was followed by nothing. What we did: we opened each Flight Centre page in a real browser so the JavaScript ran, saved that version, removed menus and clutter, and fixed prices that came out as single digits (for example "$ 1 3 8 7 *" became "$1387*"). Only Flight Centre pages were swapped; competitors stayed as captured. Result: citations rose from 39.3% to 45.9% of 290 answers, +6.6 percentage points. The 95% range is -0.3 to +14.1, so the gain is not certain. On the Melbourne to London question alone, citations went from 0 to 7 out of 10. The screenshot is from October and our capture from August, so the fares differ. 10

Slide 11

Problem: on route pages, the route, a repeated price, a caret symbol, the deals heading and the fee notice were all jumbled into one line of text. What we did: our code matched this line against a strict pattern and copied each value into labelled fields: departure, destination, starting fare stated on the page, and fees (the full fee wording, "Displayed fares exclude Online Booking Fee & Merchant Fee. Fees are applied once at checkout."). The fields were added as a "Product facts" section at the end of the page; the original text stayed. Nothing was guessed: no currency, dates or trip type, and any page that did not match the pattern exactly was left unchanged. Result: citations rose from 39.3% to 46.9%, +7.6 percentage points, with a 95% range of +2.8 to +13.1, so above zero. On the Melbourne to London question, 0 to 3 out of 10. 11

Slide 12

Problem: people ask AI assistants questions, but the page gives its facts as plain text with nothing linking them to a question. What we did: fixed rules (not the questions we test with) wrote a specific question from the page's own facts, and the answer is an exact quote from the same page, so nothing new is claimed. The rules skipped sales lines like "40 years in the business" and facts about the wrong country. Result: citations rose from 39.3% to 46.2%, +6.9 percentage points, with a 95% range of +1.0 to +13.1, so above zero. On the Melbourne to London question, 0 to 3 out of 10. 12

Slide 13

Two final fixes to the FAQ section. First, each FAQ question sat inside a clickable button, and text extraction throws buttons away, so the AI got the answers without their questions. Our code turned each button into plain text with the same words; checks confirmed only the questions were added and every answer was unchanged (429 question labels across the saved pages). Second, on 9 pages the FAQ sat below a long "Explore" travel guide, so we moved the whole FAQ section above it, with every word unchanged. These fixes were never tested on their own, so they have no separate result. They are part of the final page, which raised citations from 40.0% to 49.3% on the first run and 52.4% on the repeat run, both with 95% ranges above zero. 13

Slide 17

A summary of all six methods, each tested on its own against the same original page, where 39.3% of answers (114 of 290) cited Flight Centre. Kept: labelled facts +7.6 points (46.9%), question and answer +6.9 (46.2%), showing hidden deals +6.6 (45.9%, range includes zero). Not used: schema code plus a visible sentence +6.2 (45.5%; both changed together, and Google says no special markup is needed for its AI features), outside facts +3.4 (42.8%, range includes zero), AI rewriting -5.5 (33.8%, range entirely below zero). The kept changes were refined and combined with two FAQ fixes into the final page, which raised citations from 40.0% to 49.3% and 52.4% across two runs. The final page used refined versions, so the individual gains cannot be added together. 17

Slide 18

Is the increase enough? It is enough to show the page changes worked inside our sandbox, but not enough yet to prove the business outcome. What supports it: both runs of the final page beat the original with 95% ranges above zero (+3.1 to +15.9 and +5.2 to +20.0 points). The gain is larger than the gap between two runs of identical pages (+3.1 points, range -2.8 to +8.3). In the repeat run, 21 of 29 questions improved, 6 declined and 2 stayed the same. In relative terms, 23% and 31% more answers cited Flight Centre. Our sandbox also reproduced two effects that published research reports: moving Flight Centre's source to the top of the list added 18.3 points, matching the position effects in Lost in the Middle (TACL 2024) and C-SEO Bench (NeurIPS 2025); and automatic AI rewriting lowered citations, matching C-SEO Bench's finding that many rewriting methods hurt. That suggests the sandbox behaves the way independent studies say answer engines behave. (The GEO paper's "up to 40%" uses a different measure, the share of answer words, so we do not compare it directly.) What it does not prove: we have not tested live AI engines, traffic or bookings. In the open-contest check, where the system picks its own sources, citations barely changed (6.50% to 6.75%). Our aspirational target was 60%; the best run reached 52.4%. The final page was also chosen after development, and the baseline was historical, so the result is likely somewhat optimistic. Our design could only reliably detect gains of about 9 to 11 points or more. So the decision is a governed live pilot, not a full rollout. 18

Slide 19

What we learned. Exact percentages will change as models change, but five lessons should hold. 1. AI can only cite what it can read. Deals that load with JavaScript and FAQ questions inside buttons were invisible in the text the AI received; making them readable helped. Vercel found the AI crawlers from OpenAI, Anthropic and Perplexity do not run JavaScript. 2. Substance beats style. Adding real, labelled facts and question-and-answer structure helped (+7.6 and +6.9 points), while automatic AI rewriting lowered citations (-5.5). Google's May 2026 guidance also says content does not need to be rewritten for AI, and C-SEO Bench found many stylistic rewrites do not help. 3. Getting into the list matters most. Moving Flight Centre's source to the top added 18.3 points with the text unchanged, the largest effect we saw, and when the system picked its own sources there was no gain. So the next lever is discovery: classic SEO that gets Flight Centre's pages retrieved in the first place. 4. Measure how often, not where. The same question gave different answers each run, so we counted the share of ten answers citing Flight Centre. SparkToro found the same: how often a brand appears is far more stable than its position. 5. Re-test when models change. Our sandbox can swap models and recapture search results, so a fresh, fair re-test is cheap. 19
